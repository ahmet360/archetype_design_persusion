## Overview

Pop Art is an art movement that emerged in the 1950s in Britain and the United States and drew its themes and techniques from popular mass culture, such as advertising and comic books (Encyclopaedia Britannica, n.d.). It began in London in the mid-1950s with artists such as Richard Hamilton and then flourished in 1960s America with Andy Warhol and Roy Lichtenstein. It is considered postmodernist because it treats everyday consumer imagery as worthy of art and blurs the line between "high" art and "low" commercial culture.

## Key characteristics

- **Imagery from ads, comics and celebrities:** The subject matter comes from everyday media and consumer life, such as product packaging, comic strips, magazine ads and famous faces.
- **Repetition and mass production:** Images are repeated, often in grids or series and with printing methods such as silkscreen, so the work looks like it came off an assembly line.
- **Irony and the blurring of high and low culture:** Pop Art mixes fine art with commercial imagery and takes a playful, self-aware tone that can both celebrate and question consumer culture.

You can often spot Pop Art by its recognizable brand or media imagery, its repeated motifs and its flat, graphic, printed look.

## Brand or website example

**[Coca-Cola](https://www.coca-cola.com/)**

Coca-Cola is one of the best-known examples of consumer culture treated as art. Andy Warhol painted the brand's bottle in works such as *Green Coca-Cola Bottles* (1962), using repetition to show a mass-produced product as an icon. Its website carries that spirit into the present. It uses a bold red brand color, a modular layout of repeated promotional cards, and a rotating hero banner that places the product in the center of the page. Slogan-style headlines such as "Refresh Your Fandom" and "Paint the Nation" and its pop-culture and sports tie-ins keep the tone short, catchy and playful, like an ad.

## Application to web design

- **Layout:** Use comic-panel or tiled grids to break content into bold, distinct blocks. Use repeating modules for products or features, so the repetition gives the page rhythm. Let a few elements, such as stickers, callouts or badges, overlap or break out of the grid to add energy.
- **Typography:** Treat slogans like ad copy, with large outlined or stacked text for headlines and short taglines. Keep it to a few key phrases.
- **Color:** Use high-contrast color blocking, with large blocks of saturated color behind content, to separate sections and make the page feel bold.
- **Imagery:** Use bold illustration and vector graphics with flat color and clear outlines in place of realistic photography.
- **Content and tone:** Write short, punchy, slogan-style copy in a playful, ironic or humorous voice. Use pop-culture references where they fit the audience, but keep key information, such as prices, instructions and policies, in plain language.
- **User decisions:** The style grabs attention quickly, builds a fun, youthful brand personality and makes calls to action obvious, because bold colored buttons stand out against color-blocked sections.
- **Accessibility:** Respect users' reduced-motion preferences by limiting or disabling animation, such as moving banners, for people who ask for it.
